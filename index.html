Pharmacology of Analgesics

Updated 2024–2025 Based on WHO, BNF & IASP guidelines

WHO Analgesic Ladder

The WHO three-step analgesic ladder (1986, revised concept 1996) provides a sequential, evidence-based framework for pain management. Originally designed for cancer pain, it is now applied broadly to acute and chronic pain. The guiding principles are: by the mouth (prefer oral route), by the clock (regular dosing, not PRN), by the ladder (stepwise escalation), and for the individual (tailored titration).

I
Mild Pain (NRS 1–3) Non-opioid: Paracetamol and/or NSAID ± Adjuvant
II
Moderate Pain (NRS 4–6) Weak opioid (Tramadol, Codeine) ± Non-opioid ± Adjuvant
III
Severe Pain (NRS 7–10) Strong opioid (Morphine, Buprenorphine, Fentanyl, Oxycodone) ± Non-opioid ± Adjuvant

Clinical Note — Step II Controversy Added

Some contemporary pain guidelines (EAPC, 2012) suggest that Step II weak opioids add limited benefit and can be bypassed in favour of low-dose strong opioids (e.g., low-dose oral Morphine 5–10 mg q4h) when moderate-to-severe cancer pain is inadequately controlled by Step I drugs. The "two-step" approach is gaining traction in palliative care literature.

Paracetamol / Acetaminophen

Paracetamol Panadol · Calpol · Disprol · Febrol Step I

Mechanism: The precise mechanism remains debated. The predominant view is central inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis via the endocannabinoid and serotonergic pathways, with weak action on a putative COX-3 isoform. Unlike NSAIDs, paracetamol is inactivated peripherally by tissue peroxides, explaining its negligible anti-inflammatory effect at therapeutic doses. It also acts on descending serotonergic pain-inhibitory pathways.

Dosing
  • Adults (healthy): 500 mg–1 g PO q4–6h — Max 4 g/day
  • Elderly / Hepatic disease / Alcohol use / Low body weight (<50 kg): Consider a maximum of 3 g/day according to regulatory guidance.
  • Severe hepatic impairment or decompensated liver disease: Many specialists conservatively limit dosing to 2 g/day.
  • IV Paracetamol (Perfalgan): 1 g IV over 15 min q6h — bioavailability advantage over oral in post-op setting
  • Paediatric: 15 mg/kg q4–6h PO/PR — Max 5 doses in 24h; not to exceed 75 mg/kg/day

Hepatotoxicity — NAPQI Toxicity

In overdose, hepatic glucuronidation and sulphation pathways are saturated. CYP2E1/CYP3A4 converts excess paracetamol to NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine), a highly reactive electrophile that depletes hepatic glutathione, causing centrilobular necrosis and fulminant liver failure.

Antidote: N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) IV — most effective within 8–10 hours of ingestion but beneficial up to 24 hours. Standard regimen: 150 mg/kg IV over 1 h, then 50 mg/kg over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg over 16 h (Rumack-Matthew nomogram guides treatment threshold).

Paracetamol Safety in Pregnancy Updated

Paracetamol has historically been considered the safest analgesic in all trimesters. However, a 2021 consensus statement (CARES) and several observational studies suggest a possible association between prolonged prenatal paracetamol exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes (ADHD, autism spectrum). The FDA and EMA (2023) advise using the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration in pregnancy, though they do not recommend avoidance for acute pain management.

NSAIDs

Mechanism: Competitive, reversible inhibition of cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2), blocking conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins (PGE₂, PGI₂), thromboxane A₂ (TXA₂), and other eicosanoids. This produces analgesic, antipyretic, and anti-inflammatory effects. Note: Aspirin irreversibly acetylates COX-1 (and COX-2), distinguishing it from other NSAIDs.

NSAID Contraindications & Adverse Effects

GI Toxicity: PGE₂ protects gastric mucosa; COX-1 inhibition → ulcers, erosions, GI bleeding. Co-prescribe a PPI (omeprazole 20 mg OD) for high-risk patients (age >65, prior PUD, concurrent anticoagulation). Misoprostol is an alternative mucoprotectant.

Renal Impairment: Prostaglandins maintain afferent arteriolar tone under low-perfusion states. NSAIDs reduce GFR; risk of acute kidney injury in dehydration, elderly, or pre-existing CKD.

Cardiovascular Risk: COX-2 selective agents reduce PGI₂ (vasodilatory/antithrombotic) without reducing TXA₂ (vasoconstrictive/pro-thrombotic), tipping the balance toward thrombosis. Naproxen has the lowest CV risk among non-selective NSAIDs.

AERD (Aspirin-Exacerbated Respiratory Disease): ~10% of asthmatics. COX-1 inhibition shunts arachidonic acid to the lipoxygenase (5-LOX) pathway, producing excess leukotrienes. Avoid all NSAIDs; celecoxib may be cautiously used after supervised challenge.

Ketorolac Dosing Clarification Fact-Checked

The original 30 mg IM dose for all adults has been revised. Current BNF and FDA guidelines recommend 10 mg IV/IM q6h for elderly patients (>65 yrs) or those <50 kg. The systemic use limit of 5 days (combined parenteral + oral) is a hard regulatory limit due to risk of GI bleeding and renal injury with prolonged use. Ophthalmic preparations (0.4%–0.5% drops) are not subject to this restriction.

Opioids

Mechanism: Opioids act as agonists at G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs): Mu (μ), Kappa (κ), and Delta (δ). Mu receptors mediate the primary analgesic effects but also respiratory depression, euphoria, and physical dependence. Activation causes pre-synaptic reduction of cAMP and closure of voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; post-synaptically, K⁺ channels open, hyperpolarising the neuron and inhibiting pain transmission at spinal and supraspinal levels.

Weak Opioids (Step II)
  • Tramadol Tramal · Tonoflex · Ultram — Dose: 50–100 mg PO/IV q6h (Max 400 mg/day; 300 mg/day in elderly)
    Dual mechanism: weak Mu agonist + inhibition of serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake. Active metabolite O-desmethyltramadol (M1) has 200× higher Mu affinity than parent drug; M1 production is CYP2D6-dependent.
  • Codeine Codipar · Tylenol with Codeine Added — Dose: 30–60 mg PO q4–6h (Max 240 mg/day)
    A prodrug; requires CYP2D6 conversion to morphine (~10% of dose) for analgesic effect. Ultra-rapid metabolisers risk morphine toxicity; poor metabolisers (<1% efficacy). Contraindicated in children <12 and post-tonsillectomy patients.
  • Nalbuphine Kinz · Nalbin — Dose: 10–20 mg IV/IM/SC q3–6h
    Mixed agonist-antagonist: Kappa agonist (analgesia, sedation) + Mu partial antagonist. Has a "ceiling effect" for respiratory depression, making it safer than pure Mu agonists.
  • Buprenorphine Temgesic · Subutex (SL) · Butrans (patch) Moved from Strong — Partial Mu agonist with very high receptor affinity and slow dissociation. Also a Kappa antagonist. Analgesic doses: 200–400 mcg SL q6–8h or 5–20 mcg/h TD patch (q7 days). Exhibits a ceiling on respiratory depression.

Tramadol Safety Risks

Seizure Risk: Tramadol lowers seizure threshold via SNRI mechanism. Avoid in epilepsy, head injury, or concurrent use with SSRIs/SNRIs/MAOIs/TCAs.
Serotonin Syndrome: Triad of agitation, autonomic instability, and neuromuscular abnormalities (clonus). Highest risk with MAOIs — treat with benzodiazepines and cyproheptadine (serotonin antagonist).
CYP2D6 Polymorphism: Poor metabolisers (∼7–10% Europeans) get no analgesia; ultra-rapid metabolisers risk toxicity. Avoid in known ultra-rapid metabolisers, especially breastfeeding mothers.

Nalbuphine Caveat — Precipitated Withdrawal

Because it functions as a Mu antagonist, Nalbuphine will precipitate acute opioid withdrawal in patients physically dependent on or currently taking pure Mu agonists (e.g., Morphine, Fentanyl, Oxycodone). It should never be administered concurrently without careful assessment of opioid dependence status. This also applies to Buprenorphine — induction must occur during mild-to-moderate withdrawal (COWS score ≥8) to avoid precipitated withdrawal.

Strong Opioids (Step III)
  • Morphine MST Continus · Oramorph · Zomorph — Dose: 10 mg IV/IM q4h or 15–30 mg PO q4h (IR); 30–60 mg PO q12h (SR/MR formulation). Gold-standard Mu agonist. Active metabolite Morphine-6-Glucuronide (M6G) accumulates in renal failure — dose reduction required.
  • Fentanyl Sublimaze · Duragesic patch — Dose: 25–100 mcg IV q1–2h or TD patch 12–100 mcg/h q72h. Highly lipophilic (100× more potent than morphine IV); rapid onset (<1 min IV), short duration (30–60 min IV). No active renal metabolites — preferred in renal failure.
  • Oxycodone OxyContin · Oxynorm Added — Dose: 5–10 mg PO q4–6h (IR); 10–40 mg PO q12h (CR). Oral bioavailability ~60–87% (higher than morphine). Available as oxycodone/naloxone (Targin) combination to reduce opioid-induced constipation.
  • Hydromorphone (Dilaudid) Added — Dose: 1–4 mg PO q4–6h; 0.2–1 mg IV/SC q2–4h. ~5× more potent than morphine (oral). Useful in renal failure (fewer active metabolites than morphine); minimal histamine release.
  • Methadone Physeptone Added — Complex pharmacokinetics; long variable half-life (8–150h). Also an NMDA antagonist — useful in neuropathic and opioid-tolerant pain. QTc prolongation risk — baseline ECG essential.

Adverse Effects

Respiratory depression (most dangerous; dose-dependent), constipation (no tolerance develops — prophylactic laxatives from day 1), nausea/vomiting (tolerance develops within 1–2 weeks), miosis (pupillary constriction persists — no tolerance), urinary retention (especially in BPH), pruritus (histamine release, or centrally mediated), tolerance, physical dependence, and hyperalgesia (OIH — with prolonged use).

Opioid Reversal — Naloxone Protocol

Antidote: Naloxone (Narcan) — Competitive Mu antagonist. Dose for respiratory depression: 0.04–0.4 mg IV q2–3 min titrated to adequate ventilation (avoid abrupt reversal causing acute withdrawal or flash pulmonary oedema). Max 10 mg total.

Critical Half-Life Warning: Naloxone half-life is only 30–90 min. All opioids (except some IR formulations) outlast naloxone. The patient must be monitored for re-narcotisation for at least 4–6 hours after the last naloxone dose, or a naloxone infusion initiated (two-thirds of the effective bolus dose per hour). IM naloxone 0.4 mg can be used if IV access is unavailable.

Intranasal Naloxone Added: 4 mg/0.1 mL intranasal (Narcan Nasal Spray) is now approved for community use and opioid overdose first response; onset 8–13 min.

Local Anesthetics

Mechanism: Reversible blockade of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels from the intracellular side of the nerve membrane. The drug must first cross the membrane in its unionised (lipid-soluble) form, then re-ionise intracellularly to block the channel. Therefore, acidic environments (infected tissue, pH <7.0) reduce efficacy by trapping the drug in its ionised (membrane-impermeable) form — this is why LA is less effective in abscesses.

Epinephrine (Adrenaline) Adjunct Added

Vasoconstrictors (1:100,000–1:200,000 epinephrine) slow systemic absorption, prolonging block duration and reducing peak plasma concentration. Contraindicated in ring blocks of digits, penis, nose, ear (end-arteries — risk of ischaemic necrosis). Also avoid in patients on MAOIs or non-selective beta-blockers due to hypertensive crisis risk.

Ropivacaine vs Bupivacaine Added

Ropivacaine is a pure S(-)-enantiomer, producing less cardiac and CNS toxicity than racemic bupivacaine. It preferentially blocks Aδ and C fibres over motor fibres at lower concentrations — useful for analgesia without motor blockade in labour epidurals (0.1–0.2%). Bupivacaine is still preferred for surgical spinal anaesthesia due to denser block.

Local Anaesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)

Caused by accidental intravascular injection or overdose exceeding maximum safe dose. Progresses through CNS toxicity then cardiovascular collapse.

CNS Prodrome: Tinnitus, perioral numbness, metallic taste, light-headedness, visual disturbances → seizures → CNS depression.
Cardiovascular: Widened QRS, VT/VF, severe bradycardia, cardiovascular collapse (bupivacaine most cardiotoxic due to lipophilicity and slow unbinding).

Management: Stop injection immediately. Call for help. Airway management (100% O₂). BLS/ACLS as needed. Antidote: IV Lipid Emulsion 20% (ILE) — Bolus 1.5 mL/kg over 1 min, then infusion 0.25 mL/kg/min.
Avoid in LAST: Vasopressin, CCBs, Beta-blockers, and Lidocaine (for arrhythmia). Propofol is NOT a substitute for ILE (insufficient lipid content, worsens haemodynamic instability).

Specific Clinical Scenarios

Acute & Post-Operative Pain

Utilise Multimodal Analgesia (MMA) — combining drugs from different classes targeting multiple mechanisms to achieve superior analgesia while minimising opioid-related adverse effects (ERAS principle). A standard MMA regimen may include: regular Paracetamol 1g q6h + NSAID (if no contraindication) + wound site infiltration with LA + opioid PRN for breakthrough pain. Studies show MMA reduces opioid consumption by 20–60%.

Cancer & Chronic Pain

Follow the WHO Analgesic Ladder. Use long-acting / extended-release opioids for baseline pain (e.g., MST Continus 12-hourly) supplemented with short-acting IR opioids PRN for breakthrough pain (breakthrough dose = 10–15% of total daily opioid dose). Reassess regularly; titrate upward if requiring >3–4 breakthrough doses/day.

Neuropathic Pain

Standard analgesics (NSAIDs, Paracetamol) are generally ineffective as monotherapy. First-line treatments (NeuPSIG 2015 guidelines): Gabapentinoids, TCAs (amitriptyline/nortriptyline), or SNRIs (duloxetine/venlafaxine). Topical Lidocaine and Capsaicin are first-line for localised neuropathic pain (e.g., post-herpetic neuralgia). Tramadol and strong opioids are second- or third-line due to risk-benefit profile.

Acute Migraine Management Added

Step 1 (mild-moderate): Aspirin 900 mg + metoclopramide 10 mg (antiemetic and improves gastric motility) OR Ibuprofen 400 mg OR Naproxen 500 mg.
Step 2 (moderate-severe or Step 1 failure): Triptans (5-HT₁B/D agonists) — Sumatriptan 50–100 mg PO or 6 mg SC. Avoid in uncontrolled hypertension, IHD, or vasospastic angina.
Avoid opioids for acute migraine — they increase risk of medication-overuse headache (MOH).

Acute Gout Management Added

First-line: High-dose NSAID (e.g., Indomethacin 50 mg TID × 5 days or Naproxen 500 mg BID × 5 days). Alternatively Colchicine 1 mg then 0.5 mg 1h later (BNF low-dose regimen, as effective as high-dose with fewer GI SE). Oral prednisolone 30–35 mg OD × 5 days if NSAIDs/Colchicine are contraindicated. Ice packs and joint rest provide adjunct relief.

Procedural / Procedural Sedation Pain Added

Sub-dissociative ketamine (0.3–0.5 mg/kg IV over 15 min) provides potent analgesia while preserving airway reflexes and spontaneous respiration — useful in the Emergency Department for fracture reduction, wound care, and pelvic exams. Adjunctive midazolam (1–2 mg IV) reduces dysphoric emergence reactions.

Special Populations & Drug Interactions

PopulationKey Considerations
Elderly (>65 yrs) Principle: "Start low, go slow." Increased susceptibility to NSAID-induced GI bleeding and acute kidney injury (reduced renal reserve). Highly sensitive to opioid-induced sedation, confusion, and falls. The Beers Criteria (AGS 2023) recommends avoiding indomethacin and ketorolac, and using caution with long-acting benzodiazepines combined with opioids. Paracetamol is the preferred first-line analgesic. Updated
Renal Failure (CKD / AKI) Avoid all NSAIDs (reduce GFR, worsen fluid retention). Gabapentinoids and Tramadol require strict dose reduction (renally cleared). Morphine accumulates active metabolites (M6G) — avoid or reduce dose significantly. Preferred opioids: Fentanyl and Buprenorphine (no renally excreted active metabolites). Paracetamol is safe. Methadone is complex — specialist input required. Updated
Hepatic Failure Limit Paracetamol to max 2 g/day (mild-moderate disease). In severe hepatic failure (Child-Pugh C) — avoid entirely. Avoid NSAIDs: risk of GI bleeding (coagulopathy), hepatorenal syndrome, and fluid retention. Opioids accumulate due to reduced first-pass metabolism and protein binding — use with extreme caution; Alfentanil and Fentanyl have shorter context-sensitive half-lives and may be preferable IV.
Pregnancy Paracetamol: Still the safest for acute pain — use minimum effective dose, short-term. Guidance evolving (see above).
NSAIDs: Use with caution in 1st/2nd trimester; strictly avoid from 20 weeks onwards (FDA warning 2020 — risk of fetal renal dysfunction, oligohydramnios, and premature ductus arteriosus closure in 3rd trimester). Updated
Opioids: Short-term use acceptable under supervision. Chronic use → Neonatal Opioid Withdrawal Syndrome (NOWS). Avoid long-acting formulations near delivery if possible.
Paediatrics All dosing must be weight-based (mg/kg). Aspirin contraindicated <16 years due to Reye's syndrome risk (mitochondrial dysfunction → hepatic encephalopathy + cerebral oedema, often after viral illness). Codeine and Tramadol contraindicated <12 years (EMA/FDA 2013–2015) due to unpredictable CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolism causing respiratory depression. Codeine also contraindicated for post-tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy analgesia up to age 18. Ibuprofen is safe from 3 months of age. Updated
Substance Use Disorder Added Opioid-dependent patients have altered pain sensitivity (opioid-induced hyperalgesia). Avoid abrupt opioid discontinuation. If on opioid agonist therapy (methadone, buprenorphine), continue maintenance dose and add short-acting opioids for acute pain (may require higher doses). Maximise non-opioid multimodal analgesia. Engage addiction specialist where possible.
Obesity / Bariatric Surgery Added Avoid routine dose-calculation by total body weight for opioids — use adjusted body weight (IBW + 0.4 × excess weight) for IV opioids. Avoid oral sustained-release formulations post-bariatric surgery (unpredictable absorption due to shortened GI tract). Increased OSA risk — reduce opioids; use regional techniques and non-opioid analgesia where possible.

Critical Drug Interactions Added

Opioids + Benzodiazepines/CNS depressants: FDA Black Box Warning (2016). Concomitant use increases risk of profound respiratory depression and death. Monitor closely; use only when no alternatives exist.

Tramadol + SSRIs/MAOIs: Serotonin syndrome risk. Avoid co-prescription; if essential, use extreme caution and monitor for triad (hyperthermia, agitation, clonus).

NSAIDs + Anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs): Increased bleeding risk (GI tract and displacement from plasma proteins). Use topical NSAIDs or paracetamol preferentially.

NSAIDs + ACE inhibitors/ARBs + Diuretics: "Triple Whammy" combination — high risk of AKI. Avoid concurrent use, especially in the elderly or those with reduced renal reserve.

Methadone + QTc-prolonging drugs: Additive risk of Torsades de Pointes. Check and monitor ECG regularly.
